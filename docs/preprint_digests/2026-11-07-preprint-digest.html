A comparative transcriptomic framework across kidney organoid, assembloid, and transplantation models

Glibetic, N.; Lee, K. C. Y.; Barnhill, J.; Ortega, M. A. | Bioinformatics | 2026-11-05 | organoid

This research aimed to understand how well different lab-grown kidney organoids mimic human kidney development and function. The scientists analyzed gene activity from many kidney organoid studies using single-cell and single-nucleus transcriptomics, comparing them to real fetal and adult kidneys. They found that while some organoid methods create more adult-like cells in certain kidney parts, none fully replicate mature adult proximal tubules. The study also highlighted that using only fetal or adult kidney data for comparison can be misleading, and that organoids grown in combination with other tissues or transplanted show specific characteristics. These findings offer guidance for choosing the best kidney organoid models for research and development.

Lineage tracing in rhabdoid tumor organoids and orthotopic xenografts reveals convergent transcriptional states

Perticari, G.; DeMartino, J.; Fumagalli, A.; Ayyildiz, D.; Doulkeridou, S.; Kester, L.; Kooiman, J.; Margaritis, T.; Dro... | Cancer Biology | 2026-11-04 | organoid

This study investigated whether the varied gene activity observed in aggressive pediatric tumors called malignant rhabdoid tumors (MRTs) arises from different cell populations or from cells changing their behavior. Researchers used a technique to track cell lineages and single-cell RNA sequencing in lab-grown MRT models and in animal studies. They found that tumors and cultures narrowed down to a few dominant cell lineages, and these expanding lineages had distinct gene activity patterns. Importantly, different lineages converged on similar gene activity patterns, suggesting a limited set of available states, and these patterns remained consistent even when tumors spread. The study concludes that while the specific outcome of which cell lineages become dominant is variable, the available gene activity patterns are drawn from a consistent and recurring set.

Bodnia, E.; Basart, M.; Hai, S.; Ford, L.; Miolane, N.; Kosik, K. S.; Bouwmeester, D.; Carr, L. D. | Neuroscience | 2026-11-06 | organoid

This study investigated whether neural activity in organoid brain models exhibits a low-dimensional structure that can be detected by a mathematical technique called persistent homology. Researchers analyzed recordings of neural activity from human and mouse cortical organoids by creating networks based on correlations between neurons. They discovered that the data revealed significant "loop" structures (H1) in the neural activity patterns, which persisted even when some neurons were removed. The findings suggest that persistent homology can effectively identify underlying organizational principles in neural recordings at the scale typically obtained in experiments.
