Single-cell lineage tracing of human neuromesoderm organoids reveals TBX6-mediated posterior mesoderm fate diversification

Liao, Y.; Chen, C.; Li, M.; Wang, C.; Wang, L.; Zhu, M.; Yao, Y.; Peng, G. | Developmental Biology | 2026-07-30 | organoid

This study aimed to understand how different cell types in the human posterior body develop from a specific type of stem cell called neuromesodermal progenitors (NMPs). Researchers used a sophisticated genetic tool (CRISPR/Cas9) to track individual cell lineages and combined it with detailed molecular analysis (scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq) in lab-grown human organoids. They discovered that intermediate and paraxial mesoderm arise from a common precursor, while lateral plate mesoderm separates earlier. The findings also show that a gene called TBX6 is crucial for the development of intermediate mesoderm and somites, and its absence prevents proper maturation of these tissues. This research provides a detailed map of human NMP development and sheds light on congenital disorders affecting the spine and kidneys.

This study investigated how genetic changes in BRD4 lead to developmental problems, even though BRD4 is usually known for helping genes turn on. Therapeutic acetaminophen exposure does not measurably alter human cortical organoid development

Trovo, L.; Kucera, G.; Gomez, G.; de Souza, J. S.; Muotri, A. R.; Iakoucheva, L. M. | Neuroscience | 2026-07-29 | organoid

This study investigated how acetaminophen, a common pain reliever used during pregnancy, affects the development of the human brain before birth. Researchers used lab-grown brain organoids derived from human stem cells and exposed them to acetaminophen at levels similar to those experienced in pregnancy. They found that acetaminophen did not alter the organoids' size, structure, or cell survival, nor did it impact neuronal development or the formation of connections between brain cells. While some minor gene expression changes were observed at the highest dose, overall, the study suggests that acetaminophen at typical prenatal exposure levels does not significantly disrupt early human brain development.

This study investigated how the location and movement of mRNA molecules change as human stem cells differentiate into specific cell types. This work offers a new tool to understand how mRNA targeting contributes to development and disease.

This study investigated why expanded CAG repeats in the Huntingtin gene cause Huntington's Disease, focusing on the role of regions surrounding the repeat. Researchers used genome-edited mini-organoids and neurons to compare human and mouse versions of the gene's exon 1. They discovered that the human version was more toxic to neurons than the mouse version, and that a specific region called the proline-rich domain (PRD) was responsible for this difference. Replacing the human PRD with the mouse PRD reduced the harmful effects, and this was linked to restoring the function of the actin cytoskeleton and a protein called MKL2/MRTFB.
