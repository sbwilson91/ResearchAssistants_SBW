This study investigated why mutations in the NPHP1 gene cause nephronophthisis, a kidney disease leading to kidney failure. Researchers used CRISPR gene editing to create kidney organoids from human stem cells that either had normal NPHP1 or lacked it. They found that organoids without NPHP1 had damaged kidney structures, lost important kidney cells, and were unable to repair DNA damage. SOX2-dependent enhancer activation couples epithelial identity to neuronal maintenance

Raman, S.; Dubey, A.; Prakash, A.; Kaushik, R.; Kannan, L.; Chugh, P.; Ladher, R. K. | Developmental Biology | 2026-08-21 | organoid

This study investigated how sensory neurons in the ear remain dependent on supporting epithelial cells for survival and guidance, even after they develop from the same initial cell population. Researchers found that the gene SOX2 plays a crucial role in maintaining this connection by ensuring the epithelium continues to produce essential molecules. Specifically, when SOX2 was removed from the ear's epithelial cells after neuron development, the neurons' axons failed to reach the epithelium, and the neurons began to die. This was linked to SOX2 controlling genes in the epithelium that promote neuron growth and guide their axons, such as Ntf3 and Bdnf. The findings suggest that SOX2 helps maintain the communication between developing neurons and their supporting epithelial cells, highlighting how regulatory processes can continue even as cell types become more specialized.

Diverse Intestinal Injuries Drive Heterogeneous Transcriptional Responses and Limited Reactivation of Developmental Gene Programs in Human Enteroids

This study investigated how the human intestine responds to damage, specifically whether it reactivates developmental gene programs like the mouse intestine does. Researchers used human intestinal organoids and exposed them to various injury conditions, analyzing the activation of key signaling pathways. They found that while most injuries led to common responses like P53 activation and reduced cell division, only butyrate treatment successfully triggered a human developmental gene signature. The study also identified that P53 promotes an adult gene signature, while TGFB and YAP pathways encourage a developmental one. These findings help understand different human intestinal injury responses and suggest how signaling pathways can be targeted to protect the intestinal lining.

This research aimed to uncover the cause of a common type of DNA mutation (SBS17) found in stomach and esophageal cancers. Scientists used genetic experiments in cell cultures and organoids, along with whole-genome sequencing, to investigate this. They discovered that SBS17 is not caused by oxidative damage as previously thought, but rather by errors during DNA replication where a molecule called dUTP is incorrectly inserted.
