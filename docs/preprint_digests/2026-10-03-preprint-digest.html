Cerebral organoids recapitulate interneuron chain migration in the human cortex

This research investigates how nerve cells called interneurons migrate into the human brain after birth, a process that cannot be studied in living rodents. Scientists used long-term human brain organoids, grown for over six months, to observe this migration in real-time. They discovered that interneurons move in chains, and that support cells called astrocytes help organize these chains into larger streams. This study also found that genes linked to neurodevelopmental disorders are particularly active in these migratory streams, and that altering two specific genes, CNTN5 and FGF14, disrupted the migration process. These findings highlight the utility of brain organoids for understanding complex human brain development and its connection to diseases like autism.

This research investigated how different human tissues respond to a lack of mechanical load, simulating microgravity. Scientists used rotating-wall vessels to culture neural, cardiac, and tonsil organoids for 24 hours. They found that these organoids showed tissue-specific gene expression changes similar to those observed in actual spaceflight, with neural and cardiac organoids appearing "older" transcriptomically. Additionally, cardiac organoids exhibited altered beating patterns, and tonsil organoids showed changes in their antiviral gene activity. This work provides a new way to study how multiple organs react quickly to reduced gravity.
