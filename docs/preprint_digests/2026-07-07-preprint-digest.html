Klf4 overexpression remodels chromatin to reprogram late retinal progenitors toward an retinal ganglion cell-like fate

Oliveira-Valenca, V. M.; Roberts, J. M.; Chang, F.; Bosco, A.; Vetter, M. L.; Silveira, M. S. | Neuroscience | 2026-07-02 | scRNA

This study investigated how to overcome limitations in creating new retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) for therapies. Researchers found that overexpressing a gene called Klf4 in late retinal progenitor cells, which normally can't become RGCs, led to cells with some RGC characteristics. Using advanced sequencing techniques, they discovered that Klf4 alters gene activity and the structure of the cell's DNA, guiding these progenitor cells towards an RGC-like identity. This work suggests Klf4 could be useful in regenerative treatments for eye conditions by enabling RGC development outside the usual developmental period.

This research investigates how the gut microbiome can make otherwise unresponsive colorectal cancers susceptible to immune therapies. Colorectal cancers with distinct metastatic potential trigger divergent early T cell responses

Saad, M.; Thoms, A.; Yin, Y. S.; Mamede, I.; Rahman, A.; Chen, R.; Carrasco, S. E.; Darcy, P. W.; Goto, N.; Tavazoie, S.... | Immunology | 2026-07-04 | organoid

This study investigated how T cells respond to different types of colorectal tumors, aiming to understand why immunotherapy often fails. Researchers used lab-grown colorectal tumors and tracked T cells over time to see how they behaved. They found that non-spreading tumors attracted T cells that became active and could kill cancer cells, while tumors likely to spread caused T cells to remain inactive. T cells interacting directly with tumor cells were more active and capable of killing. Ultimately, T cells from non-spreading tumors helped slow tumor growth, but those from spreading tumors did not, partly because the spreading tumors reduced a key immune signaling molecule. These findings suggest that a failure of the immune system to engage effectively locally happens before cancer spreads.

This study aimed to understand the early molecular causes of Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) by examining the immune cells in patients before they received treatment. Researchers created a detailed map of immune cells from intestinal biopsies of IBD patients and healthy individuals using single-cell sequencing and analyzed how these cells communicate. They found that both types of IBD showed an increase in inflammatory monocytes, and a specific communication pathway involving Galectin proteins, particularly Galectin-9, was central to the disease.
