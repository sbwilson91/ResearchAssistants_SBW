The contradiction in this week's Garmin data is worth naming directly: HRV is LOW at 68 ms, yet training readiness scores 75/100 (HIGH). That's not unusual in the days after an ultra — the autonomic nervous system is still stressed from the accumulated effort (hence low HRV), but Garmin's readiness model, which factors in low acute load and adequate sleep-quality signals, reads the reduced training demand and returns a high score. The honest read is: system is recovering, not yet ready to race. Low HRV here is a non-training stressor signal — the ultra itself — not something requiring action beyond what's already happening. Sleep, however, is a genuine flag: 5.6h average with a 2.5h worst night and a sleep score of 59.8/100 sits right at the edge of the threshold that suppresses adaptation. With Copenhagen 14 days out, poor sleep is the one thing that could undermine the fitness banked in August.

VO₂ max at 55.2 ml/kg/min and declining slightly (-0.2 over two weeks) is an expected post-ultra artifact — don't read this as a fitness loss. The Garmin HM prediction of 1:33:36 sits 3:36 from the sub-1:30 target, and at face value that looks like a gap. But Garmin's model doesn't account for race-day taper sharpness, and historically this athlete's real race performances have tracked close to predicted times. The more honest framing: the sub-90 proof session was supposed to be the 17 Aug long run with 10 km at goal pace. Whether that was executed determines whether the fitness is genuinely banked — the prediction model is a secondary signal. The aerobic efficiency reading this week (5:44/km at 130–145 bpm, 11 s/km faster than the prior 8-week average) is a small but real positive: the legs are already returning.

No threshold or quality session was detected this week that would yield cadence data, so there's nothing to assess from the speed metrics. Vertical oscillation at 8.8 cm (88 mm) remains above the 80 mm target, but weekly average oscillation on easy recovery runs has limited diagnostic value — it's influenced heavily by the low effort level and short durations. The real test will come in next week's quality session, where the combination of cadence and oscillation under load will tell whether the posterior chain is recovering adequately. Given the calf endurance history and the fact that the ultra involved significant accumulated ground contact, the first hard session back deserves a conservative approach to volume within the workout — let the calves signal their readiness rather than assuming they're fine.
