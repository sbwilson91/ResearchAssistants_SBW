This study investigated whether high levels of 25-hydroxycholesterol (25-HC), a substance produced by immune cells, make kidneys more vulnerable to statin-induced damage. Researchers found that statins combined with 25-HC caused kidney cell death in lab experiments and led to kidney injury in mice. The mechanism involved a disruption in a cellular cleaning process called autophagy, which was reversed by adding a substance that restores protein prenylation or by reducing the immune cells producing 25-HC. This study investigated how different ways of measuring neural complexity relate to the underlying structure of brain networks. Researchers used computer simulations of neural activity, systematically altering the network's connections to create varying degrees of integration and differentiation. They found that measures of complexity based on spontaneous activity were inconsistent, reflecting different aspects of the network structure. However, measures based on how the network responds to external stimuli consistently showed the highest complexity in networks with a balance of local and widespread connections, similar to a waking brain state. This suggests that perturbational complexity measures are more reliable indicators of brain network architecture.

This study investigated whether individual human lymph nodes maintain distinct collections of memory T cells, even within the same drainage areas. Researchers analyzed lymph nodes from different parts of the body, along with spleen and lung tissue, from human organ donors. They found that while immune cell composition varied between major regions, lymph nodes draining the lungs showed very similar collections of memory T cells and their genetic makeup. While T cells specific to certain viruses were found more in their respective drainage areas, the overall memory T cell populations in paired lung-draining lymph nodes were remarkably alike.
