A comparative transcriptomic framework across kidney organoid, assembloid, and transplantation models

Glibetic, N.; Lee, K. C. Y.; Barnhill, J.; Ortega, M. A. | Bioinformatics | 2026-11-05 | organoid

This research aimed to understand how well different lab-grown kidney organoids mimic human kidney development and function. Scientists analyzed gene activity from various organoid studies, comparing them to real fetal and adult kidneys. They found that some organoid methods better replicate specific kidney cell types, like podocytes and distal tubules, especially when assembled into larger structures or transplanted. However, no current method fully replicates mature adult proximal tubules, and the choice of reference (fetal or adult kidney) significantly impacts how organoid cells are classified. These findings offer guidance for researchers choosing the best organoid models for their specific studies.

Lineage tracing in rhabdoid tumor organoids and orthotopic xenografts reveals convergent transcriptional states

Perticari, G.; DeMartino, J.; Fumagalli, A.; Ayyildiz, D.; Doulkeridou, S.; Kester, L.; Kooiman, J.; Margaritis, T.; Dro... | Cancer Biology | 2026-11-04 | organoid

This research investigated whether the diverse gene activity seen in aggressive pediatric cancers called malignant rhabdoid tumors (MRTs) arises from different cell populations growing, or from cells changing their behavior. Scientists used a method to track cell lineages and single-cell gene activity in lab-grown MRT models and in animal studies. They found that over time, tumors narrowed down to a few dominant cell lineages, and these expanding lineages had distinct gene activity patterns. Interestingly, different lineages could adopt similar gene activity patterns independently, and these patterns remained stable even when the cancer spread. Ultimately, while the available gene activity patterns are limited and recurring, which specific lineages become dominant can vary.

Researchers developed a new method to analyze DNA methylation from very small amounts of DNA and used it on individual stem cell-derived clones in a mouse model where a cancer gene could be turned on.
