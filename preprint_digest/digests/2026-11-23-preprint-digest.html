A Standalone PD-1 Chimeric Switch Receptor T-Cell Platform Expands the Therapeutic Window of PD-L1 Targeting in Solid Tumors

This research aimed to develop a safer way to target PD-L1, a molecule found on cancer cells and immune cells that can suppress anti-tumor responses. The scientists created a new type of T-cell therapy using a "chimeric switch receptor" (CSR) that specifically targets PD-L1. This new approach showed strong killing of cancer cells and immunosuppressive myeloid cells, but significantly reduced harm to normal tissues like lung, brain, kidney, liver, and heart cells. The engineered T-cells were effective in both lab-grown 3D tumor models and in animal studies, suppressing tumor growth and extending survival. This work suggests a promising new strategy for PD-L1 targeted therapies with improved safety.

Neural rosettes emerge from an interconnected tubular network in human cortical organoids

Trovo, L.; Gomez, G.; Einhorn, A.; Muotri, A. R.; Iakoucheva, L. M. | Neuroscience | 2026-11-18 | organoid

This research investigates how neural rosettes, structures found in lab-grown brain organoids, actually form. Scientists used fluorescent nanoplastics to track the process, observing neuroepithelial folding and internalization. They discovered that rosettes arise from a single, interconnected tubular network, not as separate units. This finding offers a new understanding of early brain development and suggests brain organoids could be useful for studying birth defects affecting the neural tube.

This study investigates how the primate cerebral cortex expanded by examining the development of neural progenitors. Researchers compared gene activity and accessibility in the developing brains of humans, macaques, and mice. They discovered two specific developmental programs enriched in humans that are linked to outer radial glia (oRG) and maintaining progenitor cells. A gene called RHOJ was found to be particularly active in oRG cells and more abundant in human brains, and manipulating it in mice affected progenitor numbers and neuron types. This suggests that changes in how existing genes are used, rather than entirely new genes, contributed to the evolution of human brain development.

This study investigated how human-specific genetic elements, called transposable elements (TEs), contribute to the development of the human brain. This study investigated whether transplanting human retinal organoids could help protect and restore vision after optic nerve injury. Researchers transplanted lab-grown retinal organoids into mice with crushed optic nerves and observed the effects over several weeks. They found that the transplants helped preserve the structure of the retina and reduced the loss of retinal ganglion cells and their connections. While the organoids didn't significantly improve overall retinal function, they did lead to better activity in the visual pathway and improved visual behavior in the mice. This suggests that retinal organoid transplantation could be a promising approach for treating optic nerve damage and restoring some visual function.

This study investigated the usefulness of single-cell foundation models (FMs) for tasks like identifying cell types and predicting gene regulatory networks. This study aimed to develop a better laboratory model to understand intracerebral hemorrhage, a severe type of stroke. Researchers used human brain organoids, which are lab-grown brain tissues, and made them vascularized to mimic the brain's blood vessels. They then used a drug called atorvastatin to weaken these blood vessels and simulated bleeding by adding human blood. Common organization of collective dynamics across divergent self-organized neural architectures

This research investigates how the physical structure of brain-like networks influences their electrical activity. Scientists compared two types of lab-grown neural networks: one with a layered, cortex-like structure and another with cell clusters connected by nerve fibers.
