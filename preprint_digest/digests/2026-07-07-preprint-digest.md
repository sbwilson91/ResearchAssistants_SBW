> This research investigates how the gut microbiome can make otherwise unresponsive colorectal cancers susceptible to immune therapies. ### [Colorectal cancers with distinct metastatic potential trigger divergent early T cell responses](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.30.735606)
**Saad, M.; Thoms, A.; Yin, Y. S.; Mamede, I.; Rahman, A.; Chen, R.; Carrasco, S. E.; Darcy, P. W.; Goto, N.; Tavazoie, S....** | Immunology | 2026-07-04 | `organoid`

> This study investigated how T cells respond to different types of colorectal tumors, aiming to understand why immunotherapy often fails. Researchers used lab-grown colorectal tumors and tracked T cells over time to see how they behaved. They found that non-spreading tumors attracted T cells that became active and could kill cancer cells, while tumors likely to spread caused T cells to remain inactive. T cells interacting directly with tumor cells were more active and capable of killing. Ultimately, T cells from non-spreading tumors helped slow tumor growth, but those from spreading tumors did not, partly because the spreading tumors reduced a key immune signaling molecule. These findings suggest that a failure of the immune system to engage effectively locally happens before cancer spreads.
