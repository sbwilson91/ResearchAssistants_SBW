Lineage-aware stochastic modeling reveals gene-expression dynamics in development and disease

Xing, J.; Staklinski, S. J.; Liu, Z.; Nowak, D.; Siepel, A. | Bioinformatics | 2026-06-28 | scRNA

This research addresses how gene expression changes over time within developing cell lineages, which is often missed by current methods that treat cells as static. The scientists developed a new probabilistic framework called LaVOUS that uses lineage information and models of biological processes to analyze single-cell RNA sequencing data. Their findings show that LaVOUS is better than existing methods at identifying gene expression changes linked to cell lineages and can accurately reconstruct the history of gene expression. This work is significant because it provides a more powerful tool for understanding how gene expression evolves during development and disease.

This study investigated how a specific mutation in the TDP-43 protein, linked to ALS and FTD, leads to neurodegeneration. Researchers created brain organoids with this mutation and observed that it caused TDP-43 to become abnormally phosphorylated and accumulate in the cell's cytoplasm. Gene expression identifies regional central nervous system vulnerability to heat

Pagni, S.; Bouchama, A.; Sisodiya, S. | Neuroscience | 2026-06-26 | organoid

This study investigated why Purkinje neurons in the cerebellum are particularly vulnerable to damage during heatstroke. Researchers analyzed gene expression data from various human heat exposure scenarios and brain models. They found that heat stress generally reduces cellular activity, but the cerebellum showed less of this reduction compared to other brain regions. Specifically, Purkinje neurons were strongly associated with genes that were less suppressed by heat, and these genes are important for neuron function. The study also identified a potential drug that might counteract the molecular changes seen in heatstroke.

This study aimed to find the best human photoreceptor cells for treating retinal diseases by comparing cells derived from different stages of development. Researchers used cells grown in a lab from human stem cells, at 120, 150, or 200 days of development, and transplanted them into mice with a genetic eye condition. They found that photoreceptor cells from all tested stages survived long-term, integrated structurally into the mouse retina, and formed connections with host cells. Crucially, these transplanted cells showed signs of functional integration, as evidenced by light-evoked responses in the host's retinal neurons. This suggests that human stem cell-derived photoreceptors hold promise for restoring vision in retinal disorders.

Peroxisome dynamics during HSV-1 life cycle in human neurons

Filipponi, C.; De Carli, A.; Gane, I.; Pignata, C.; Iacono, E.; Filippini, F.; Sciandrone, G.; Favaro, D.; Wesesky, M. A... | Microbiology | 2026-06-24 | organoid

This study investigated how the herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) affects the metabolism of nerve cells, particularly in relation to Alzheimer's disease. Researchers found that HSV-1 hijacks the cell's peroxisomes, which are involved in lipid metabolism, to boost its own replication. They observed that the virus causes peroxisomes to grow and change shape, leading to an increase in specific lipids that the virus uses to build its outer layer. These changes were seen in different types of human nerve cells and brain models, and were crucial for the virus to multiply. The findings suggest that this virus-induced metabolic rewiring of peroxisomes could make nerve cells more susceptible to damage in neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer's.

Organoid-in-Bead (OrB): vortex-based compartmentalization enables scalable, high-density intestinal organoid culture

Hattori, K.; Kirisako, H.; Matsuo, M.; Ota, S. | Bioengineering | 2026-06-23 | organoid

This research addresses the challenge of using intestinal organoids for large-scale studies, which is currently limited by inefficient traditional culture methods. The scientists developed a new technique called Organoid-in-Bead (OrB) that uses vortexing to create thousands of tiny Matrigel beads, each containing an organoid fragment. This method allows for high-density, scalable organoid culture from a single preparation, maintaining organoid quality and enabling expansion. The OrB system significantly reduces the amount of Matrigel and medium needed per organoid, making it a practical and efficient approach for generating a large number of intestinal organoids for screening purposes.

This study investigated how malaria-related parasites interact with their bird hosts at a cellular level. Researchers used single-cell RNA sequencing on blood samples from infected birds to analyze both parasite and host gene activity. They found that the parasites employ strategies to evade the host's immune system and potentially influence its response, but the host's immune cells showed little activation, suggesting the parasite suppresses immunity.
