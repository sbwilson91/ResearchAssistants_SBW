> This research investigates how the gut microbiome can make otherwise untreatable colorectal cancers respond to immunotherapy. Scientists used mouse models to show that a microbiome containing *Helicobacter* can slow tumor growth and prevent spread. This protection is linked to more immune cells entering the tumor and becoming active, which happens because the bacteria cause cancer cells to display more immune-recognition molecules (MHC class II). This discovery suggests a new way to potentially treat colorectal cancer by manipulating the microbiome to boost the immune system's attack on tumors and improve responses to existing immunotherapies.


### [Colorectal cancers with distinct metastatic potential trigger divergent early T cell responses](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.30.735606)

> This study investigated how T cell responses differ between non-metastatic and pro-metastatic colorectal cancers. Researchers used transplanted CRC organoids and tracked T cell behavior over time. They found that non-metastatic tumors attracted T cells that became active and capable of killing cancer cells, while pro-metastatic tumors resulted in T cells that remained largely inactive. Importantly, T cells interacting directly with non-metastatic tumor cells were more active and cytotoxic, and these T cells helped slow tumor growth. The findings suggest that a failure of the immune system to effectively engage with early tumors contributes to their ability to spread.
