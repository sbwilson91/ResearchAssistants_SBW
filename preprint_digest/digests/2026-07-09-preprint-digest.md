### [Same-section spatial metabolo-transcriptomics using Stereo-meta-seq reveals DHA-driven kidney maturation](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.16.732541)
**van Scheppingen, R. H.; Wu, B.; Zhang, Y.; Schep, S.; Winter, U.; Wang, F.; Zou, Y.; Chuva de Sousa Lopes, S. M.; Silver...** | Cell Biology | 2026-07-08 | `organoid`

> This study investigated whether local molecules in developing tissues simply follow along or actively guide tissue development. To answer this, researchers developed a new method called Stereo-meta-seq, which combines two advanced techniques to map both molecules and gene activity within the same tissue sample at a very detailed level. They found that a specific fat molecule, docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), was concentrated in developing kidney tubules and that DHA helped these tubules mature by activating specific genes. This work suggests that fats play a direct role in guiding kidney development and provides a new tool for studying how molecules and genes work together in tissues.

### [Regulation of protein abundance in neurons by selective translation of 3'UTR isoforms](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.08.737200)
**Gorey, S.; Ozbulut, H. C.; Carrasco, J.; Zhang, Y.; Hess, A.; Akol, I.; Alfonso-Gonzalez, C.; Shi, M.; Grzejda, D.; Wolt...** | Molecular Biology | 2026-07-09 | `organoid`

> This study investigates how neurons control protein production, as problems with this process are linked to memory issues and neurodegenerative diseases. Researchers found that longer 3' untranslated regions (3'UTRs) in neuronal messenger RNAs (mRNAs) make them less efficiently translated into proteins compared to shorter versions. This regulation involves a feedback loop where the long 3'UTR of the ELAV protein limits its own production, and ELAV is involved in extending the 3'UTRs of many other neuronal genes. These extended 3'UTRs are then targeted by Pumilio, which further reduces their translation, thereby maintaining stable protein levels. Disrupting this mechanism harms animal survival and development, suggesting it's a conserved way to ensure proper protein balance in specific cell types.

### [Evolutionary diversification of lipid logistics shapes synaptic maturation in primates](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.08.734953)
**Rava, V.; Restelli, E.; Mirabella, F.; Silvestrini, L.; Cannone, E.; Ciuba, K.; Abbas, M.; Graziadei, A.; Francolini, M....** | Evolutionary Biology | 2026-07-08 | `organoid`

> This study investigates why the human brain takes so long to develop compared to other primates. Researchers compared human and chimpanzee brain cells and organoids, looking at the fats in their cell connections (synapses). They found that human neurons have fewer and differently arranged vesicles at their synapses, suggesting a slower release of signals. While human neurons mature more slowly and have fewer connections, they surprisingly have more of a specific type of fat called gangliosides, which are important for organizing proteins at synapses. These findings suggest that changes in how fats are used at synapses might explain the prolonged development of the human brain.


### [Species-specific basal fluidization shapes early forebrain development](https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.10.07.680907)
**Xu, S.; Kim, S.; Li, G.; Wilsch-Braeuninger, M.; Kolodziejczyk, A.; Schutze, T. M.; Albert, M.; Trivedi, V.; Campas, O.;...** | Developmental Biology | 2026-07-08 | `organoid`

> This study investigates the physical processes behind the larger surface area of the human forebrain compared to other apes. Researchers used oil microdroplets to measure tissue movement and mechanics in brain organoids from humans, gorillas, chimpanzees, and mice. They discovered that only human brain tissue experiences a temporary "fluidization" in its lower layer, characterized by increased cell movement and rearrangement. This fluidization, driven by nuclear activity and wider cell spacing, appears to help expand the brain's surface by allowing new nuclei to move to the base, a key feature of human brain development.


### [Regulation of FGF/MAPK signaling at the primary cilium base controls the proliferation-neurogenesis balance in human cerebellar organoids](https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.04.14.648790)
**Brunetti, L.; Wiegering, A.; Anselme, I.; Vu, M. N.; Pollara, L.; Catala, M.; Saulnier, O.; Antoniewski, C.; Valente, E....** | Developmental Biology | 2026-07-08 | `organoid`

> This study investigated how problems with primary cilia, tiny hair-like structures on cells, affect the development of the human cerebellum. Researchers used lab-grown human cerebellar organoids with genetic mutations linked to Joubert syndrome, a condition affecting brain development. They found that these mutated organoids had fewer crucial nerve cells called Purkinje cells, along with problems in nerve cell creation and too much cell division. This was linked to an overactive FGF/MAPK signaling pathway, and a key part of this pathway was found to build up at the base of the primary cilia. Importantly, blocking this signaling pathway helped fix the cell division and Purkinje cell issues, suggesting the primary cilium plays a role in controlling this important developmental signal.

**Geerlings, C.; Darmasaputra, G.; Jordan Ortiz, C.; Chuva de Sousa Lopes, S. M.; Clevers, H. M.; Galli, M.** | Cell Biology | 2026-07-08 | `organoid`

> This study investigates why polyploid cells, which have extra sets of chromosomes, can be beneficial in some tissues but harmful in others. Researchers compared naturally polyploid cells in worms and human organoids with artificially polyploid human cells. They used imaging and cell-cycle tracking to measure cell size and protein production in both diploid and polyploid cells. The findings revealed a consistent pattern: as cells become polyploid, their size and protein-making ability increase proportionally, regardless of whether this polyploidy is normal or induced. This suggests a fundamental cellular response to increased genome copy number that could explain the different roles of polyploidy.
