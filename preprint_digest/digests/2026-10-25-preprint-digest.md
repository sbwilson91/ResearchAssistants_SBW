> This study investigated whether high levels of 25-hydroxycholesterol (25-HC), a substance produced by immune cells, make kidneys more vulnerable to statin-induced damage. Researchers found that statins combined with 25-HC caused kidney cell death in lab experiments and led to kidney injury in mice. The mechanism involved a disruption in a cellular cleaning process called autophagy, which was reversed by adding a substance that restores protein prenylation or by reducing the immune cells producing 25-HC. > This study aimed to understand how West Nile virus (WNV) infects human brain cells, as previous models didn't accurately reflect human brain tissue. Researchers used a human cerebral organoid model and analyzed infected cells using single-cell RNA sequencing. They discovered that WNV primarily infects progenitor cells, immature neurons, and astrocytes, while mature neurons are mostly resistant. The findings suggest that astrocytes are key in the immune response, and that a specific metabolic pathway involving the gene PHGDH is crucial for WNV to infect these susceptible cells. This work identifies PHGDH as a potential target for new antiviral therapies, with the existing drug disulfiram showing promise in reducing WNV replication.

> This study investigated how different ways of measuring neural complexity relate to the underlying structure of brain networks. Researchers used computer simulations of neural activity, systematically altering the network's connections to create varying degrees of integration and differentiation. They found that measures of complexity based on spontaneous activity were inconsistent, reflecting different aspects of the network structure. ### [Human lymph node CD8+ T cell memory is nested within drainage regions](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.10.19.760021)
**Cai, C.; Ko, Y.; Berg, E.; Wild, N.; Östholm, M.; Ringqvist, E.; Sutevski, I.; Marques, R. C.; Kaewkam, N.; Bonaiti, E....** | Immunology | 2026-10-23 | `organoid`

> This study investigated whether individual human lymph nodes maintain distinct collections of memory T cells, even within the same drainage areas. Researchers analyzed lymph nodes from different parts of the body, along with spleen and lung tissue, from human organ donors. They found that while immune cell composition varied between major regions, lymph nodes draining the lungs showed very similar collections of memory T cells and their genetic makeup. While T cells specific to certain viruses were found more in their respective drainage areas, the overall memory T cell populations in paired lung-draining lymph nodes were remarkably alike. Importantly, T cells enriched in lung-draining lymph nodes still responded well to influenza stimulation, suggesting that regional location doesn't solely determine a T cell's ability to be reactivated.
