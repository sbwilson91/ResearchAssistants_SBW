This study investigated how sensory neurons in the ear remain dependent on supporting epithelial cells for survival and guidance, even after they develop from the same initial cell population. Researchers found that the gene SOX2 plays a crucial role in maintaining this connection. By removing SOX2 from the ear's epithelial cells after neuron development, they observed that neuron axons couldn't reach the epithelium and the neurons subsequently died. This suggests SOX2 in the epithelium is essential for providing signals that guide and support the neurons. The research identified that SOX2 controls genes within the epithelium that produce growth factors and molecules important for axon guidance, such as Ntf3 and Bdnf. Ultimately, this work highlights how SOX2 ensures ongoing communication between developing sensory cells and the neurons they support.

Mutagenic bypass of uracil-derived abasic sites underlies the SBS17 mutational signature

This study investigated the cause of a common type of DNA mutation (SBS17) found in stomach and esophageal cancers, which previously had no clear explanation. Researchers used genetic experiments in cell cultures and organoids, along with whole-genome sequencing, to uncover the mechanism. They found that SBS17 mutations are not caused by oxidative damage as previously thought, but rather by errors during DNA replication where a molecule called dUTP is incorrectly inserted. This is then repaired in a way that leads to mutations, and this process is responsible for both natural mutations and those caused by chemotherapy. Understanding this mechanism is significant because it explains a major source of mutations in these cancers and could lead to new ways to prevent cancer progression and drug resistance.
